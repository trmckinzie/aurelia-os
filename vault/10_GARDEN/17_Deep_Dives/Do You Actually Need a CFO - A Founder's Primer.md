# Do You Actually Need a CFO? A Founder's Primer

*A ~10-minute read on what a startup CFO actually owns, what it costs, and why the honest answer at 10–30 people is usually "not yet"*

---

## The most misunderstood title on your org chart

Ask ten founders what a CFO does and you'll get ten answers, because the title has been stretched to cover everything from a part-time bookkeeper who "handles the finances" to an executive who runs your Series B. That elasticity is an expensive problem when you're running a company of ten to thirty people. You can end up paying executive money for clerical work, or — more common and more damaging — asking whoever currently does your books to produce the financial model your lead investor is about to pick apart. Neither mistake announces itself. Both surface at the worst possible moment, usually about three weeks into a raise.

The confusion is structural, not personal. "CFO," "controller," "accountant," and "bookkeeper" get used interchangeably in conversation even though the underlying job descriptions are genuinely distinct, and every one of those roles is sold to founders by firms that would happily place any of them. Kruze Consulting, which provides all four levels of service to venture-backed startups, draws the line where most practitioners do: controllers exist to make the numbers accurate and compliant, while CFOs take those numbers and turn them into strategy, capital allocation, and growth decisions ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/controller-vs-cfo/)).

This piece resolves three things: what a startup CFO actually owns at seed and Series A, what that person does week to week, and what it costs to buy full-time versus fractionally. It ends with the case for not hiring one at all yet.

## What a startup CFO actually owns

Pull apart real seed and Series A job descriptions and the scope clusters around six things.

**Fundraising narrative and investor relations.** CFOs build the fundraising materials, maintain the relationships with investors and lenders, and negotiate terms ([Wellfound](https://wellfound.com/blog/startup-cfo-job-description)). Kruze describes the role as the company's primary liaison to its investors ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/controller-vs-cfo/)) — meaning the CFO is often the person answering the diligence question you don't want landing in the founder's inbox at 9pm.

**Financial modeling and forecasting.** The model an investor will actually stress-test, and which internally drives hiring, pricing, and growth decisions ([Volopay](https://www.volopay.com/blog/cfo-roles-and-responsibilities-in-startup/)). This is the single most portable artifact a CFO produces.

**Board reporting.** Once you have institutional board members, someone owns monthly board reporting *with commentary* — not a spreadsheet export, but an explanation of what moved and why ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-startup)).

**Cash runway management.** Tracking burn closely enough to know, on any given day, how many months you have ([Milestone](https://milestone.inc/blog/what-does-the-cfo-do-in-a-startup)).

**Strategic and unit-economics work.** Evaluating performance and surfacing opportunities for growth, optimization, and risk mitigation ([Wellfound](https://wellfound.com/blog/startup-cfo-job-description)) — translating operating data into decisions leadership can actually make.

**Running the raise itself.** At many companies the CFO doesn't just support fundraising, they own the process end to end once a round is imminent ([Volopay](https://www.volopay.com/blog/cfo-roles-and-responsibilities-in-startup/)).

Now the hard line beneath all of that. A **controller** owns the mechanics that make the CFO's numbers trustworthy: month-end and year-end close, journal entries and account reconciliations, GAAP-compliant financial statements, plus direct oversight of accounts payable, accounts receivable, and the general ledger, along with internal controls and audit prep ([4 Corner Resources](https://www.4cornerresources.com/job-descriptions/controller/); [NetSuite](https://www.netsuite.com/portal/resource/articles/accounting/financial-controller-vs-cfo.shtml)). A **staff accountant** does the day-to-day recording — payroll runs, bank and credit-card reconciliations, invoice processing, general ledger maintenance, and tax filing prep under supervision ([Insight Global](https://insightglobal.com/blog/staff-accountant-job-description-salary-and-more/); [Randstad](https://www.randstadusa.com/job-seeker/career-advice/job-profiles/staff-accountant/)). A **bookkeeper** sits at the base: recording transactions, reconciling statements, keeping AP and AR current ([Xero](https://www.xero.com/us/guides/what-is-bookkeeping/what-does-a-bookkeeper-do/); [QuickBooks](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/r/bookkeeping/bookkeeper-job-description/)). LMC's version of the ladder is the cleanest one-line summary: bookkeepers record, controllers supervise and set policy and own tax compliance, CFOs use the resulting data for strategic decisions and investor relations ([LMC](https://lmcas.com/key-role-differences-between-bookkeeper-controller-cfo/)).

None of the bottom three roles own fundraising narrative, board strategy, or capital allocation. And a CFO job description does not include reconciling a bank statement or filing a 1099. Payroll-tax compliance and worker classification in particular route to a CPA or controller function, because the IRS exposure there is mechanical and ongoing — not the kind of thing a strategic-finance hire is staffed to watch daily ([TC Advisors CPA](https://tcadvisorscpa.com/startup-tax-compliance-guide/)).

## A day-to-day picture

Early on — pre-Series A — the work is mostly defensive. As one guide puts it, at seed the CFO job is largely "do not run out of cash," with the emphasis on burn tracking and fundraising prep ([ScaleUp Finance](https://www.scaleup.finance/article/startup-cfo-when-and-why-to-hire-one)). In practice that means time in the model: updating the cash forecast, checking a proposed hire's fully loaded cost against runway before you make the offer, assembling a data room ahead of investor conversations.

Once you have an institutional board, a monthly rhythm appears. The controller closes the books; the CFO takes those closed numbers, builds the board deck, writes the commentary, and then fields investor questions in the weeks between meetings ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-startup)). By Series A the person is also underwriting the hiring plan — modeling whether a role is affordable given current burn — and managing runway precisely enough that the board is never surprised ([CRV](https://www.crv.com/content/when-to-hire-a-cfo)).

Around fundraises, the workload spikes and then collapses. Kruze advises engaging CFO help at least three months before a round because of the preparation involved, and notes that once the round closes, an interim CFO's workload "decreases dramatically" back to quarterly board reporting and KPI benchmarking ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/the-role-of-a-startup-cfo)). That spike-and-trough shape is the single most important fact about this role, and it's what drives the next section.

## The hiring decision: full-time vs. fractional

The thresholds companies actually use cluster reasonably tightly. CRV frames fractional support as relevant around $1M–$3M ARR to prepare for fundraising, with full-time finance leadership typically necessary around $8M ARR, and operating without one past $15M–$20M ARR creating real exposure ([CRV](https://www.crv.com/content/when-to-hire-a-cfo)). KORE1 puts the flip point higher: "the typical inflection point is $10M-$25M ARR, or Series B — whichever comes first," at which point revenue recognition gets complex and audit prep becomes a serious annual project ([KORE1](https://www.kore1.com/fractional-cfo-for-startups)). GetExact frames the fractional window as $1M–$20M in revenue, calling it premature below $1M and full-time territory above $20M ([GetExact](https://getexact.com/should-i-hire-fractional-cfo-save-50-percent)). Kruze's funding-stage version: part-time CFOs work well through Series C, and "past Series D, a startup will usually hire a CFO to manage the now growing accounting department" ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/saas-cfo-hire)).

Headcount corroborates this. Carta's data shows companies closing seed rounds average 5.3 employees and Series A companies average 15.6 ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/startup-compensation-h1-2024/)) — which means a 10–30 person company is squarely in the range where fractional is the norm, not a compromise.

Kruze offers the most useful conversion test, and it isn't a metric: you know it's time to go full-time when your interaction with your part-time CFO becomes constant. Hire too early and you get someone "busy during the fundraise, but who will then become an overpaid Controller for the next 2-3 years" ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/saas-cfo-hire)).

**Full-time cost.** One 2026 compensation report puts seed-stage CFO pay at $100,000–$175,000 base ($120,000–$200,000 total) with 1.0%–2.0% equity, Series A at $175,000–$275,000 base ($200,000–$325,000 total) with 0.5%–1.0%, and Series B at $250,000–$350,000 base ($300,000–$425,000 total) with 0.25%–0.7%, on standard four-year vesting with a one-year cliff ([GetExact](https://getexact.com/startup-cfo/)). Another benchmark runs higher on cash: seed $200,000–$275,000 total comp with 1–2% equity, Series A $250,000–$325,000 with 0.5–1.5%, Series B $300,000–$400,000 ([StartupCFO.ai](https://www.startupcfo.ai/insights/startup-cfo-salary-by-stage)). Kruze's blunt rule of thumb — "a full time CFO will run you 240K+" ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/the-role-of-a-startup-cfo)) — sits between them. All-in, counting benefits and recruiting, GetExact estimates $250,000–$500,000+ against a three-to-six-month time-to-hire ([GetExact](https://getexact.com/should-i-hire-fractional-cfo-save-50-percent)). For scale: a full-time controller averages $119,497 with a $97,000–$160,500 range, and a full-time bookkeeper averages $50,573 ([ZipRecruiter, Controller](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Salaries/Controller-Salary); [ZipRecruiter, Bookkeeper](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Salaries/Bookkeeper-Salary)).

**Fractional cost.** Hourly rates run $175–$500 with a median around $300 ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/fractional-cfo-hourly-rates-2026-benchmarks)); one pricing guide bands them as $175–$250 entry-level, $250–$350 mid, and $350–$450 senior ([Compass App](https://compassapp.ai/guides/how-to-price-fractional-cfo-services-in-2026)). Most practitioners now prefer monthly retainers. Compass App quotes $3,000–$15,000 per month across three scope tiers ([Compass App](https://compassapp.ai/guides/how-to-price-fractional-cfo-services-in-2026)); OpsFi, drawing on K38 Consulting data, puts the typical small and mid-market retainer at $5,000–$7,500 ([OpsFi](https://opsfi.co/insights/fractional-cfo-cost-when-to-hire)). For seed-to-Series-A specifically, most companies pay $4,000–$10,000 per month, with hours scaling from 10–15 monthly pre-seed to 25–40 at Series A ([StartupCFO.ai](https://www.startupcfo.ai/insights/startup-cfo-salary-by-stage)). Annualized, a retainer runs roughly $96,000–$180,000 against $350,000–$550,000+ for a full-time hire ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/fractional-cfo-hourly-rates-2026-benchmarks)); OpsFi models the gap at $415,000 saved (83%) for a $5M ARR company, rising to $634,250 (79%) at $15M ([OpsFi](https://opsfi.co/insights/fractional-cfo-cost-when-to-hire)). Fractional hires typically receive no equity and no benefits, since they aren't employees ([Mercury](https://mercury.com/blog/hiring-a-fractional-CFO)).

One caveat worth carrying into negotiations: nearly all fractional fee benchmarks are published by the firms and marketplaces selling the service. There is no independent survey of fractional CFO pricing comparable to the salary data above. Treat those ranges as market-rate signals, not audited figures, and get multiple quotes.

## What you're actually buying

Strip out the tables and the choice is simple. A fractional CFO buys **judgment and process on a budget** — someone who has built the model and sat across from investors before, engaged for the twenty-odd hours a month you actually need, with no equity grant and no severance risk. Because the work spikes around raises and troughs afterward, part-time capacity matches the actual shape of the demand at your stage.

A full-time CFO buys **ownership and availability** — someone embedded deeply enough to catch a runway problem before it becomes a board surprise, present for every pricing debate and every hiring decision, accountable in a way a contractor structurally cannot be. That costs real cash and real equity, and it only pencils out once your volume of financial decisions has outgrown part-time bandwidth. Both CRV's $8M ARR mark and KORE1's $10M–$25M range are attempts to name that moment; Kruze's "your interactions have become constant" is the version you can actually feel.

What neither option buys is bookkeeping or close discipline. That's a separate purchase, and skipping it to hire up the stack is, as CFO Advisors puts it, backwards — founders who hire a fractional CFO before they have a working controller function end up paying strategic rates for someone to make the books trustworthy first ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-startup)).

## You probably don't need a CFO yet if…

You're pre-revenue or under roughly $500K ARR. At that stage your needs are basic bookkeeping, expense tracking, and maybe a simple projection for the pitch deck — a bookkeeper and a spreadsheet cover it ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-startup)).

Your bookkeeping isn't caught up. If your close takes days instead of hours, or nobody is reliably reconciling accounts each month, the fix is a bookkeeper or outsourced controller — not a CFO. A controller has to exist, in some form, before a CFO's numbers mean anything ([CRV](https://www.crv.com/content/when-to-hire-a-cfo)).

You have no raise on the horizon in the next six to twelve months. The fundraising spike is the clearest trigger there is, and Kruze's advice is to engage help about three months out — which means today's answer, with no round in sight, is not yet ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/the-role-of-a-startup-cfo)).

You have no institutional board members expecting monthly commentary, and no hiring plan complex enough to need underwriting. Those are the two operating triggers that turn strategic finance from nice-to-have into necessary ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-startup); [CRV](https://www.crv.com/content/when-to-hire-a-cfo)).

And you're small enough that a five-day month-end close would be absurd overhead against your actual transaction volume ([CFO Advisors](https://cfoadvisors.com/blog/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo-startup)).

## Part 3: The Plain-English Summary

A CFO is not the person who does your books — that's a bookkeeper. A CFO is not the person who makes sure the books are right — that's a controller. A CFO is the person who takes books that are already right and uses them to raise money, brief a board, and decide what the company can afford. If your books aren't clean yet, hiring a CFO buys you an expensive person doing a cheaper person's job.

The work has a distinctive shape: it spikes hard around a fundraise and collapses afterward. That shape is the whole argument for hiring fractionally first. You're buying roughly twenty hours a month of someone who has built the model and faced the diligence questions before, for something like $4,000–$10,000 a month at seed-to-Series-A, versus $250,000–$500,000+ all-in for a full-time hire plus one to two points of equity and a three-to-six-month search.

The thresholds people actually use: fractional starts making sense around $1M–$3M ARR, and full-time becomes necessary somewhere between $8M and $25M depending on who you ask. But the most useful test isn't a revenue number at all — it's when your conversations with the fractional CFO stop fitting into their hours. At ten to thirty people, you are almost certainly below that line, and the right sequence is books first, fractional second, full-time only when the part-time arrangement visibly stops working.

One thing to hold onto while shopping: nearly every published fractional-CFO fee benchmark comes from a firm that sells fractional CFO services. The full-time salary data has independent sources behind it; the fractional pricing does not. Treat those numbers as opening positions and collect several quotes.
