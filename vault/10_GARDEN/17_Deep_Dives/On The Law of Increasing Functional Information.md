# Nature's "Missing Law," and What the Paper Actually Claims

*A 2023 PNAS paper proposes that functional information — a measure borrowed intact from 2003 molecular biology — increases wherever selection acts on many configurations; the phrase "missing law of nature" is the authors' own, hardened into a discovery by their institution's press office.*

## Is this an accepted law of nature?

No. It is a proposal, and its authors say so in the paper itself. The abstract states that they "propose" a law of increasing functional information, and the significance statement describes the work as approaching "a potential 'missing law'" — hedged language, scare quotes included ([PNAS 120(43): e2310223120](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2310223120); full text at [PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). Nothing since has elevated it further. Within a year PNAS published a critical Letter disputing its central empirical claim, along with a formal reply from the authors ([Root-Bernstein, PNAS 121(34), 2024](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2318689121); [Wong et al., reply](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2406598121)). An idea still being argued over in letters pages is a live hypothesis, not a law in the sense that thermodynamics or electromagnetism are laws. The ScienceAlert piece that sends most general readers here — headlined "Missing 'Law of Nature' Found That Describes The Way All Things Evolve" ([ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/missing-law-of-nature-found-that-describes-the-way-all-things-evolve)) — is not wrong about the paper's existence or ambition, but "found" does work the paper never authorizes. That gap between the literature and the headline is the real subject of this piece.

## What the paper actually says

The primary source is "On the roles of function and selection in evolving systems," by Michael L. Wong, Carol E. Cleland, Daniel Arend, Stuart Bartlett, H. James Cleaves II, Heather Demarest, Anirudh Prabhu, Jonathan I. Lunine, and Robert M. Hazen, published in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* on 16 October 2023 (DOI [10.1073/pnas.2310223120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2310223120)). Nine authors, drawn from the Carnegie Institution for Science, Caltech, Cornell, and the University of Colorado Boulder, three of them philosophers rather than natural scientists.

The claim, in the paper's own words, is conditional rather than sweeping: "The functional information of a system will increase (i.e., the system will evolve) if many different configurations of the system undergo selection for one or more functions" ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). A system qualifies as evolving, on this account, when three conditions hold: it is built from components that can be arranged in combinatorially vast numbers of ways, natural processes actually generate many of those arrangements, and some process preferentially retains a small subset of them.

That retaining process is what the paper calls selection for function, and it is deliberately wider than Darwin's version. The paper identifies three sources of it. *Static persistence* is the weakest: stable configurations — a durable crystal structure, a tightly bound nucleus — simply outlast unstable ones, with no dynamics required. *Dynamic persistence* covers systems sustained by an ongoing flow of energy, where the flow is maintained by the system doing something: dissipating free energy, catalyzing its own components, regulating itself, processing information. These four are the paper's "core functions," with narrower "ancillary functions" — a single enzyme's role inside a metabolic network — nested within them. *Novelty generation* is the third: systems capable of producing genuinely new functions face selection favoring that capacity itself, an open-ended search rather than a climb toward a fixed optimum ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)).

The claimed scope is maximal. The abstract applies the law to "stars, minerals, atmospheres, and life," and the body treats stellar nucleosynthesis, Earth's mineralogical history, and the hydrocarbon chemistry of Titan's atmosphere as instances of the same underlying process, with Darwinian evolution recast as one special case of a broader natural phenomenon rather than the paradigm case ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)).

## Measuring functional information

The measure is precise, which distinguishes it from looser talk about "increasing complexity." For a system with N possible configurations, of which M(E*x*) achieve at least a specified degree of function E*x*, functional information is

**I(E*x*) = −log₂[M(E*x*)/N]**

in bits ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). The endpoints follow from the algebra. If every configuration performs the function at the minimum threshold, M = N and the information is zero — the function discriminates nothing. If exactly one configuration achieves the maximum, the value rises to log₂N bits, which is simply the information needed to specify that configuration outright.

The paper's biomolecular illustration is a short ribozyme. A sequence ten nucleotides long, drawn from a four-letter alphabet, has N = 4¹⁰ = 1,048,576 possible forms, so functional information for any function it performs is capped at −log₂(1/4¹⁰) = 20 bits ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). The ceiling is computable from combinatorics alone; the actual value is not, because it requires knowing how many of those million sequences work — a number that comes from in vitro selection experiments, not from theory.

The fully worked quantitative case is mineralogy, Hazen's own field, published as a companion study. Applying the same formula to nine chronological stages of Earth's mineral history, it reports 22.5 bits for the earliest stellar and meteoritic minerals — 27 known species against roughly 1.59 × 10⁸ chemically possible formulas — rising to 95.5 bits by the end of the Hadean (883 species against about 5.0 × 10³¹ possibilities), and reaching 138.6 bits today (roughly 9,300 known and estimated species against about 4.8 × 10⁴⁶ possibilities). The increase is monotonic across all nine stages ([Hazen et al., *PNAS Nexus* 3(7), 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae248)).

Minerals are tractable precisely because the configuration space can be enumerated: possible chemical formulas are countable given the available elements. The paper's Titan case shows the contrast. It argues that graphene's particular bonding structure would represent a small fraction of the carbon–hydrogen arrangements achievable in Titan's atmospheric chemistry, and therefore high functional information — but computes no bit value ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). The paper is candid about why. Functional information analysis, it states, "is thus not currently feasible for most complex evolving systems because of the combinatorial richness of configuration space," and even where one functional configuration is identified, "we cannot generally know whether other solutions of equal or greater function might exist" ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). Hazen has made the same concession more bluntly in interviews, saying that for most physical objects — a single living cell included — functional information cannot be calculated even in principle ([Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/why-everything-in-the-universe-turns-more-complex/)).

## Where the idea comes from

The measure is twenty years older than the law. Jack Szostak introduced functional information in a short 2003 *Nature* piece, "Functional information: Molecular messages," devising it for biopolymers because existing measures — Kolmogorov complexity, Christoph Adami's physical complexity — did not capture the fact that many different sequences can be functionally equivalent ([*Nature* 423: 689](https://www.nature.com/articles/423689a)). Hazen then generalized it with Szostak himself as co-author, in "Functional information and the emergence of biocomplexity," presented at a National Academy of Sciences colloquium and published in PNAS in 2007, which applied the identical −log₂[F(E*x*)] formula to letter strings, Avida digital organisms, and RNA aptamers ([*PNAS* 104 suppl. 1: 8574–8581](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0701744104)).

So the mathematics arriving in 2023 is sixteen years old and carries one of its originators as a co-author. What is new is the promotion: from a biocomplexity metric to a candidate physical law, presented in the paper alongside classical laws of motion and gravitation, equipped with the three-tier taxonomy of selection as its proposed mechanism, and extended in a single statement across cosmology, chemistry, mineralogy, and biology ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)).

The paper also names its own ancestors. It states that its law "bears close parallels to the previously proposed 'law of increasing complexity'" and claims to amplify and quantify that older conjecture ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). Similar territory has been worked by Stuart Kauffman's proposed fourth law of thermodynamics for non-ergodic systems ([*Entropy* 24(10), 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9601704)), by McShea and Brandon's zero-force evolutionary law, and by Eric Chaisson's energy-rate-density measure for cosmic evolution. On thermodynamics the paper is careful rather than combative: it acknowledges the apparent tension between increasing order and increasing entropy, resolves it with the standard appeal to a low-entropy initial state plus local order sustained by energy dissipation, and positions its law as a complementary second arrow of time, not a rival to the second ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)).

## Does it hold up as a law?

The strongest case for it rests on three things. It is quantitative in units, not metaphorical. Its formalism has survived peer review since 2007 without being overturned. And in the one domain where configuration space is enumerable, it has produced a real, published, monotonically increasing empirical series across nine geological stages spanning four and a half billion years ([*PNAS Nexus* 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae248)). It also generates at least one genuinely falsifiable prediction: the paper states that the presence or absence of graphene alongside simpler acetylene-chemistry products on Titan's surface "may be determined with NASA's Dragonfly mission," which "will be able to search for a broad suite of organic compounds indicative of chemical evolution on the surface, and thereby test the ideas presented here" ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)).

The case against is stronger than a hedge. Begin with measurability, conceded in the paper's own text as quoted above: outside mineral catalogues and short nucleic-acid sequences, the quantity is asserted to increase rather than observed to increase. A law whose central variable cannot be computed for the systems its headline claims are about — biospheres, organisms, technologies — is doing rhetorical rather than measurement work in exactly those cases.

Then falsifiability. The Dragonfly test is real but narrow: it probes one abiotic chemical system, not the claim that selection-driven information increase governs biological and symbolic evolution too. In a December 2024 conversation with Wong on his Mindscape podcast, the physicist Sean Carroll is reported to have pressed for an axiomatic derivation or a set of background assumptions under which the law is a theorem, noted that functional information should fall again at the heat death of the universe, and concluded that the proposal "falls short of what we would normally call a law" while remaining a worthwhile conjecture ([Mindscape 299](https://www.preposterousuniverse.com/podcast/2024/12/16/299-michael-wong-on-information-function-and-the-origin-of-life/)).

Then circularity. Because F(E*x*) is by construction the fraction of configurations meeting a threshold, and the logarithm is monotonic, I(E*x*) must rise as the threshold rises — which makes "functional information increases under selection for function" arguably a consequence of the definitions rather than a discovery about the world. Hector Zenil, an algorithmic-information theorist at King's College London, makes the technical version of this objection: the claim "amounts to a restatement of what algorithmic probability (Levin's Universal Distribution) already predicts," and without grounding in algorithmic randomness "any probabilistic claim about the emergence or frequency of configurations risks circularity or anthropic tautology" ([Zenil](https://hectorzenil.medium.com/is-a-new-law-needed-to-explain-the-worlds-complexity-40a68f494b64)).

Finally the evidence for directionality itself. In her PNAS Letter, the ecologist Meredith Root-Bernstein (CNRS / Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle, Paris) argues that evolutionary biologists understand selection as "nonagentive, non-goal-oriented, diffuse," and that the paper's language implies agents driving nature toward a goal; she adds that "the authors' undisguised anthropocentrism leads them to believe that humans are more complex, successful, and indicative of the general trends of evolution than any other example," when bacteria are by biomass and longevity evolution's most durable winners, complex lineages are repeatedly erased in mass extinctions, and retrogressive ecological succession reduces complexity outright ([PNAS 2024](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2318689121)). The authors' reply denies the teleology — "Information is no more an agent of evolution than mass is an agent of gravity or entropy is an agent of the second law of thermodynamics" — and denies the anthropocentrism: "Nowhere in our original paper did we state that humans are better examples of evolution than anything else." But it also concedes that "we should not assume that the processes leading to long-term growth in complexity are exactly analogous to biological evolution by natural selection" ([Wong et al., reply](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2406598121)) — a retreat from precisely the unifying thesis that made the original paper newsworthy.

## How "missing law of nature" became the headline

The phrase is not a journalist's invention, and tracing it is simpler than usual because both ends of the chain are on the record. It appears first in the paper's own significance statement, where the authors describe approaching "a potential 'missing law'" — a question posed in scare quotes ([PMC10614609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614609/)). It then loses its hedge in the Carnegie Institution for Science press release, issued the day the paper published, which states flatly that the paper "describes 'a missing law of nature,' recognizing for the first time an important norm within the natural world's workings," and which also quotes an anonymous PNAS reviewer calling the framework "innovative, compelling, and sound" ([release, syndicated at phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-10-scientists-philosophers-nature-evolutionary-law.html); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1004265)). Cornell's parallel release carried the same phrase in its own headline ([Cornell Astronomy](https://astro.cornell.edu/news/natures-missing-evolutionary-law-identified)).

What followed is the signature of shared-source reporting rather than independent verification: within roughly forty-eight hours, phys.org, SciTechDaily, SpaceDaily, Axios, and ScienceAlert all ran close variants of the same framing, with phys.org's version credited outright to Carnegie ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-10-scientists-philosophers-nature-evolutionary-law.html)). ScienceAlert's contribution was to convert the press release's assertion into the perfect tense — "Missing 'Law of Nature' Found" ([ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/missing-law-of-nature-found-that-describes-the-way-all-things-evolve)). None of the coverage surveyed here mentions the funding, the measurability limitation the paper states in its own text, or the possibility that an established twenty-year-old metric was being renamed rather than discovered.

On funding, the paper's acknowledgments state that studies of evolving systems "have been primarily supported by the John Templeton Foundation," with additional support from NASA's Astrobiology Institute ENIGMA team, the Carnegie Institution for Science, and a NASA Hubble Fellowship to Wong ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2310223120)); the Carnegie release repeats it ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1004265)). Templeton's stated mission centers on research at the intersection of science and questions of purpose and meaning, and it has drawn sustained criticism from scientists and philosophers for shaping which questions get asked and how results get framed for the public ([commentary, PMC10480919](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10480919); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2013/05/21/some-philosophy-scholars-raise-concerns-about-templeton-funding)). That is a documented alignment of interests worth naming, not evidence of impropriety: the authors declare no competing interests, there is no indication on the visible record that the funder chose the "missing law" phrasing, and the arguments above stand or fall on their own.

## Part 3: The Plain-English Summary

**The punchline: there is no newly discovered law of nature here. There is one 2023 paper that takes a twenty-year-old formula for measuring how rare a working arrangement of matter is, argues that this quantity tends to rise wherever selection acts, proposes that as a law — and a same-day press release turned the authors' own hedged phrase "a potential missing law" into a found one.**

What the law claims is straightforward once the jargon is unpacked. Functional information is the number of bits needed to pick out a working configuration from among all the configurations a system could take: rare-but-functional scores high, commonplace scores near zero. The claim is that this quantity increases over time in any system with enormous numbers of possible arrangements where something filters them — survival and reproduction in biology, sheer physical stability in minerals, a sustaining flow of energy in stars and cells. On that view Darwinian evolution is one instance of a much larger pattern that also covers rocks and atmospheres. The formula came from Jack Szostak in 2003 for biological molecules, and Robert Hazen — a co-author on both papers — generalized it in 2007; the genuinely new move in 2023 is calling the resulting trend a law of physics and applying it to everything at once.

Whether it stands up depends sharply on where you look. In mineralogy it works as advertised: the published numbers climb from 22.5 bits in the oldest stellar and meteoritic minerals to 138.6 bits for Earth's roughly 9,300 mineral species today, a real measurement on a real dataset. Everywhere else, the paper concedes in its own text that the quantity cannot currently be calculated, because nobody can enumerate all the possible configurations of a cell, an ecosystem, or a planetary atmosphere. Critics have pushed harder still: a published critique in the same journal argues the evidence for any universal directional increase is weak, since simple bacteria are evolution's most enduring success and complexity regularly collapses, and that "selection for function" reintroduces the goal-directed language biology works to avoid — a charge the authors formally answered while conceding that complexity growth may not be closely analogous to natural selection after all. An information theorist has argued the central result restates something algorithmic probability already predicted. A physicist who questioned the lead author directly came away unconvinced it qualifies as a law. And because functional information rises by the construction of its own definition, there is a real question whether the claim is an empirical finding or a consequence of how the terms were set up.

What to take away is a distinction rather than a verdict. The formalism is legitimate, the mineral result is genuine, and the project — looking for one vocabulary that spans stellar chemistry, geology, and life — is worth pursuing. But a proposal that is still being contested in letters pages, that its own authors describe as potential, and whose key quantity is admittedly incalculable for most of the systems it invokes, is a research program, not an established law. When you next meet a headline announcing that a missing law of nature has been found, the useful reflex is to check the paper's abstract against the university's press release. In this case the two differ by exactly one word — *potential* — and that word is the whole story.
