# ⚛️ Cognitive Hexagon

**🔗 Related:** [[The Cognitive Revolution]], [[George Miller]], [[Computational Theory of Mind]], [[Cognitive Neuroscience]], [[Artificial Intelligence]], [[Philosophy of Mind]]

---

### 💡 Definition
> A visual diagram created in 1978 for a Sloan Foundation report (often associated with George Miller and colleagues) that defines Cognitive Science as the intersection of six distinct disciplines.

- **The Metaphor:** It is the academic version of the "Blind Men and the Elephant." To understand the mind, you cannot just look at the physical brain, nor can you just look at abstract logic. You must synthesize multiple levels of analysis.
    
- **The Six Nodes:**
    
    1. **Psychology:** How does human behavior and mental processing actually work in the lab?
        
    2. **Philosophy:** What is the nature of consciousness, meaning, and knowledge?
        
    3. **Linguistics:** How is the infinite creativity of language structured and acquired?
        
    4. **Computer Science / AI:** How can we build computational models of intelligence?
        
    5. **Neuroscience:** What is the biological hardware (the "wetware") running these processes?
        
    6. **Anthropology:** How do evolution and culture shape cognition across different societies?

### 📝 Key Insight
**Marr's Three Levels of Analysis:** The hexagon works because different fields operate at different levels of explanation (as defined by David Marr): [[Computational Theory of Mind]]

- _Computational Level (What is the goal?):_ Philosophy, AI.
    
- _Algorithmic Level (What are the rules/steps?):_ Psychology, Linguistics.
    
- _Implementational Level (What is the physical hardware?):_ Neuroscience.
    
- **The Rule:** You cannot fully understand a system by looking at only one level. Knowing the rules of chess (Algorithm) doesn't tell you how a wooden knight is carved (Implementation), and carving a wooden knight doesn't teach you how to win the game.

### 🕰️ The Original Configuration (1978)

When the hexagon was first drawn, the disciplines were not perfectly equal. The original diagram featured solid lines (strong connections) and dashed lines (weak, developing connections).

- **The Core Triad:** In the early days following the **[[The Cognitive Revolution]]**, the strongest connections were between **Psychology**, **Linguistics**, and **Computer Science**. They were united by the metaphor of the mind as a "symbol-processing machine."
    
- **The Periphery:** Neuroscience had a weaker connection because, in the 1970s, brain imaging technology (like fMRI) didn't exist yet. They knew the brain was important, but they lacked the tools to map software to hardware. Anthropology was also peripheral, included primarily to ensure human cognition wasn't studied in a cultural vacuum.
    

### 📈 How the Field Has Evolved

If you were to redraw the hexagon today, the lines of gravity would look completely different:

1. **The Rise of Neuroscience:** With the invention of modern neuroimaging in the 1990s, Neuroscience exploded. It is now arguably the dominant center of gravity, pulling the field toward **Cognitive Neuroscience** (focusing heavily on biology over abstract computation).
    
2. **The AI Paradigm Shift:** Artificial Intelligence moved away from "Symbolic AI" (writing explicit logic rules) and embraced "Connectionism" (Artificial Neural Networks). Instead of programming computers to think like logicians, we now train them to mimic the biological structure of the brain.
    
3. **The Fading of Anthropology:** While still valuable, Anthropology's influence in mainstream cognitive science has shrunk significantly, often being absorbed by or replaced with **[[Evolutionary Psychology]]**.
